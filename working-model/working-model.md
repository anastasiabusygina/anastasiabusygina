# Working Model: Owner ↔ Contributor

A specification of a 1-owner / 1-contributor working configuration. Both roles are described as work with their own obligations.

This is the minimal unit. Scaling rules — delegation across multiple contributors — are described in a separate document.

---

## What this document is for

The goal of this configuration is **clarity and speed**: no ambiguity about who does what, no downtime caused by an obligation existing without the authority or means to fulfill it.

The mechanism by which this is achieved is **process determinism**: every action has a defined owner, every request has a defined recipient, every silence has a defined cost. When team composition, tooling, or scope changes, the boundaries of roles must remain readable, and responsibility must not get mixed up or shifted around.

The wording below is intentionally rigid. Rigid wording closes the ambiguity zones where ownership is unclear or contestable — the zones in which "I thought this wasn't mine" or "we're all adults, we'll figure it out" become available as moves. The sharper the boundaries, the less effort either side spends figuring out who owns what — and the more is left for the actual work.

---

## 1. Personal stays out of the workflow

The engagement is not friendship. If anyone — Owner or Contributor — is upset, tired, or woke up on the wrong side of the bed, that stays behind the scenes and out of the workflow. The exchange is money for results; both sides hold themselves to that frame.

The Contributor does not bring personal mood into the work. The Owner does not bring personal mood into decisions, requests, or feedback. Neither side absorbs the other's mood as part of the engagement.

## 2. Authority and risk are co-located, and both are work

The Owner holds decision-making authority because the Owner carries the budget and the risk. This is not a privilege; it is a role with its own obligations. Authority without the corresponding work of deciding is as defective as expertise that refuses to inform decisions.

The Owner's work includes: knowing what they want (or explicitly commissioning a consultation when they do not), engaging with expert input enough to choose between options, and making decisions in a timeframe that does not block the work.

The Contributor's work includes: executing within the scope set by the Owner, surfacing trade-offs the Owner needs to decide, and not making decisions that belong to the risk-bearer.

Decisions made by the Contributor under the cover of "this is too complex to explain" are a transfer of authority away from the risk-bearer. Decisions deferred by the Owner under the cover of "you're the expert, you decide" are the same transfer in the opposite direction. Both break the model. Authority and risk must travel together; when they separate, the system stops being deterministic.

## 3. Expertise is a tool; ownership is work; the product is Work Output

Expertise is not a commodity, it is a tool. On its own it is worth nothing. Ownership is not a status, it is work. On its own it produces nothing.

The actual product of the engagement is **Work Output**, generated by both roles together: documentation sufficient for decisions, functional and documented code, decisions recorded in the designated tools, and a process that is visible to both sides.

The Contributor produces Work Output by executing and documenting. The Owner produces Work Output by deciding, recording those decisions, and providing what the work requires. Neither role's contribution to Work Output is optional.

## 4. Decisions require shared understanding

The Owner is entitled to expert input expressed in a language they understand. The Contributor is responsible for translating between the technical and business layers — translation is part of the expert role, not a courtesy on top of it. Expertise that cannot be communicated to the decision-maker has no business value.

The Owner is responsible for engaging with that input. Refusing to consider options on the grounds of "too complex to explain" transfers the decision to the expert, who does not carry the corresponding risk. The decision then sits with someone other than the risk-bearer; the model has stopped working.

Both failures produce the same outcome: decisions made without the pairing of knowledge and accountability. The model holds only when the Contributor explains in a way that can be understood and the Owner understands enough to decide.

## 5. Silence on a blocking request produces real cost, regardless of which side is silent

A blocking request is a request whose answer is required for work to proceed. Failure to respond to a blocking request within one business day produces team downtime: the blocked side waits, the work doesn't progress, the project sits in an information vacuum.

This downtime has a real cost, even when no one names it. The Contributor may sit on idle paid hours, or lose hours they cannot bill. The Owner may carry the cost of an unfinished task that should have shipped, or pay for a Contributor who is technically working but factually waiting. How exactly that cost is distributed depends on the agreement between the specific Owner and Contributor — paid downtime, switching to another task, partial billing — that is outside the scope of this document.

What is in scope: the cost is real, it is produced by silence, and both sides act with that cost in view. The party able to unblock the other is the party obligated to respond, or to proactively signal a delay. "I cannot answer today, I will answer by end of day tomorrow" works from either side; the other side can plan around it. Simply not answering does not work from either side; the cost accrues silently.

The obligation to **proactively signal** a delay belongs to whichever side cannot deliver a quality response on time. Chasing is not part of either role.

## 6. Transparency runs through the designated tools, in both directions

All progress and all decisions that affect the work are recorded in the tools designated by the Owner. Using these tools is not a matter of personal preference; it is the mechanism by which work becomes visible. Bypassing the tools is a failure to deliver Work Output.

The Contributor records progress, blockers, and changes of state in the designated tools. The Owner records decisions, scope changes, approvals, and direction in the designated tools. Decisions made in private channels — direct messages, calls without minutes, hallway conversations — do not exist for the process. If they affected the work, they must be reflected in the tools after the fact, or they cannot be relied upon.

Transparency is two-directional by definition. One-directional transparency is reporting, not transparency, and it does not produce determinism — it produces the appearance of it.

## 7. Scope and process change through explicit channels, not through silence or unilateral action

Either side may need to change the agreed scope, process, or tooling during the work. Such changes are legitimate, but they go through an explicit channel.

For the Contributor: disagreement with a process or tool is raised explicitly — through a request for a discussion, a written objection, or an issue comment. Until the change is approved, the existing process remains in force. Silence is not a form of disagreement; it is a failure to perform. Quietly using a different tool, quietly skipping a step, or quietly shipping outside the process is a unilateral change without authority.

For the Owner: changes to scope, deadlines, or Definition of Done after a task has been accepted go through the same explicit channel — a request, a recorded decision, a renegotiation of estimate and cost. Silently extending scope ("just add this here while you're at it"), moving the deadline backward without re-estimation, or redefining Definition of Done after work has begun is a unilateral change of agreement. The Owner has the authority to change scope; the Owner does not have the authority to change it invisibly.

Silence does not constitute disagreement, from either side. Change without record does not constitute exercise of authority, from either side. Both are unilateral departures from the agreement.

## 8. Tools and information are provided with the obligation, not after it

A task is considered started when both the obligation and the means to fulfill it are in place. Means include: access, credentials, information, decisions required to proceed, and authority to engage the people whose input is needed.

If a task is assigned without the necessary means, the timeline begins from the moment the means are provided, not from the moment the task is assigned. Holding the Contributor to a deadline while withholding what the work requires is incoherent: it asks for a result that the configuration of resources does not permit.

The same holds in the small: when the Owner needs information from the Contributor to make a decision, the Contributor provides it on the same terms — completely, in time, and in a form the Owner can act on.

## 9. Processes and rules are revised through an explicit channel

The processes, tools, and rules that govern the work are not permanent. Either side may need to revise them — because conditions changed, because something doesn't work, because a better way was found.

Such revision goes through an explicit channel: a request, a discussion, a recorded decision. Either side may initiate. The party initiating revision presents the case in a language the other side can act on (rule 4 applies).

Until a revision is agreed and recorded, the existing rule stands. The Contributor does not act outside the agreed process on the grounds that they think the process is wrong. The Owner does not change the rules retroactively on the grounds that they thought of something better. Either is a unilateral change without record (rule 7 applies).

A rule that cannot be revised is dogma; a rule revised silently is not a rule. Both failure modes break the determinism the document exists to produce.

---

## What this configuration produces

When the rules above are in place, the following holds:

- **The Owner understands what the Contributor is doing.** Progress is visible through the designated tools, not reconstructed from chat or guessed at in meetings.
- **The Contributor understands what they need to do.** Goals, scope, and Definition of Done are explicit and stable. Changes to them go through an explicit channel, not through silent extension.
- **The Owner can actually decide.** Expertise is delivered in a language the Owner understands, and the Owner does the work of understanding enough to decide. Decisions sit with the party that carries the risk, not with the party that holds the technical detail or the party that holds the budget alone.
- **The Contributor can actually work.** Access, information, and authority required to fulfill the obligation are provided together with the obligation. The Contributor is not held to deadlines for work they were not equipped to begin.
- **Renegotiation is possible during the work — from either side.** Disagreement is a legitimate channel, not a violation. Either side can request a change; until approved, the existing agreement stands.
- **Work proceeds predictably.** Because every action has a defined owner, every silence has a defined cost, and every change has an explicit channel, the system does not depend on goodwill, mood, or personal relationships to function. It can be paused, resumed, scaled, or reshaped without losing coherence.

The work is **legible to both sides at all times**, and remains legible when conditions change.

## A note on respect

This document describes the engagement in terms of roles and obligations. Some readers describe a working relationship in a different vocabulary — as a matter of *respect for the person*. This section is for them: it notes that the two vocabularies describe the same structure.

Where rules are absent, the process is often described instead as "human relations." Within that description, responses are not given on time, decisions are not recorded, unclear points are not clarified. In the vocabulary of respect, this is what gets named *disrespect*: one side uses the other's time and attention without the agreement that would govern it. The structural fact and the moral word point at the same event.

Where rules are kept, this does not occur. Responding on time, recording decisions, changing agreements explicitly — in the vocabulary of respect, this is exactly what respect for the other side's time consists of. The two vocabularies do not compete. The rules and the respect are one structure under two names.

---

## Scope

This document describes the minimal unit: one Owner, one Contributor. Both roles are treated as work, with explicit obligations on both sides. When a team grows beyond this pair, delegation rules apply — obligation, authority, and hierarchical position must be transferred together, otherwise determinism breaks down. Those rules are documented separately.
